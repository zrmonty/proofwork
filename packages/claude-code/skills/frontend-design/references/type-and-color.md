# Type, color and readable identity

Use when typography, palette or theming controls the result. Start with content hierarchy and the incumbent
system; font shopping and accent changes cannot settle a confused information structure.

## Set roles before choosing families

Identify the lead, body, supporting metadata, controls and comparison values. Establish their relationships
through scale, weight, line length and spacing. A single well-used family can have more character than a
fashionable pairing with no functional distinction. Keep the user's chosen type when it serves the brief.

Try the longest supplied title, actual paragraph lengths, dates, mixed-case names, numbers and links. Avoid
hard-coded line breaks that only suit the screenshot width. Reserve uppercase and tracking for short roles
where reading survives. Secondary text may be quiet without becoming tiny or faint. Align numerical columns
and units so people compare meaning rather than uneven character widths.

Select fonts for required scripts, glyphs, weights and availability. Check license/provenance before adding
assets; do not assume a web image or font is reusable. Prefer an adequate existing family to another network
dependency. If a new face matters, test fallback rendering, final font arrival and realistic narrow wrapping.
Verify language coverage and zoom rather than assuming Latin specimens cover the product.

## Give color a job

Separate content/background surfaces, text roles, interaction emphasis, selection and semantic status. Spend
the strongest accent on meaningful attention. A small palette with clear roles is easier to extend than one
unique color per section. Decorative hues may express a chosen identity, but cannot carry state alone.

Read failure, warning, pending and success messages without their color. Add words, symbols or structure
when meaning otherwise disappears. Contrast must hold in the actual rendered combination, including muted
text, focus, disabled explanations and text over media. Use measured checks for the applicable criterion;
"looks accessible" is not a contrast result.

Build dark surfaces from role relationships rather than mechanically inverting values. Preserve hierarchy,
brand character, distinguishable borders, form states and asset legibility. Check both appearances with the
same content. A dark panel on a light screenshot does not establish a working dark theme.

## Select expression with restraint

For an author-led site, distinct headline/body rhythm and readable bylines can carry personality without
surrounding each excerpt in a panel. For a dense tool, aligned values and clear status outrank dramatic type.
Soft corners can make controls approachable while open reading regions keep the page spacious. Extreme
rounding, broad shadows or low-contrast neutral text cannot substitute for editorial decisions.

## Verify the result

Inspect preferred and fallback fonts, long content, narrow widths, relevant zoom and available themes.
Record what actually rendered and which contrast checks ran. Fix the responsible role rather than adding
per-element overrides. Accept the chosen expression when reading and state distinctions hold; do not rotate
fonts indefinitely in search of novelty.

Use [W3C's WCAG 2.2 explanations](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/) to interpret relevant text,
contrast, reflow and focus criteria. These explanations support implementation; selected checks do not amount
to a complete conformance audit.
