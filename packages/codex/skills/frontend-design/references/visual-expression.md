# Visual expression without a template

Use when the interface needs stronger identity, more restraint, purposeful imagery or a memorable detail.
Express the actual product and accepted brief. Familiar elements are legitimate; unexamined repetition is
the problem. Novelty is not an acceptance criterion for a reliable internal tool.

## Find a meaningful source of character

Start with content and use: an essay's title/byline relationship, a product's material photography, an archive's
ordering, a tool's comparison geometry or an editor's direct manipulation. Select a small number of mechanisms
that belong together. The test is whether the composition helps this audience notice, understand or act.

For a publication, a strong masthead, distinctive reading rhythm and quiet archive can carry identity into
the article. For a portfolio, the actual work can dominate before biographical promotion. For a compact
utility, direct input/result relationships may be its most convincing expression. Do not import an entire
category's landing-page sequence into a different task.

Translate references into observations about proportion, pacing, crop, density and behavior. Keep the
project's identity and asset rights. Comparing reference screenshots does not authorize copying marks,
photographs, illustration or instructional text.

## Strengthen a flat composition

Find what deserves dominance. Try a more decisive content relationship, a richer real asset, a clearer type
contrast or a meaningful use of space. Commit to one direction across primary/detail/state views. A stronger
lead beside a quieter archive creates more hierarchy than increasing every heading.

Add a signature detail where it reinforces use: a deliberate image crop, tactile selection treatment or
an instructive transition. If everything is a signature, nothing leads. Do not fabricate testimonials,
customer logos, awards, usage numbers or scientific claims to fill a familiar promotional section.

## Calm an overloaded composition

Count competing sources of attention, not just colors. Multiple large titles, persistent glows, repeated
borders, moving backgrounds and nested panels can all claim priority. Remove or subordinate the weakest
claims while preserving navigation, errors, state and primary actions. Whitespace and alignment often do
more work than another surface layer.

Cards are useful for independent comparable items; they can flatten editorial priority when every story
receives identical weight. Gradients can express an approved material or lighting idea; applying them to
unrelated regions creates no identity. Rounded controls can invite interaction; rounding every container
may consume reading space and blur grouping. Diagnose the actual consequence rather than banning a shape.

## Handle assets as product content

Use existing supplied work first when adequate. When authorized and appropriate tools exist, create or
source needed imagery with provenance; otherwise use an honest placeholder and state the missing asset.
Do not hotlink an arbitrary search result or silently insert private assets into a public product.
Design crop, focal point, resolution and alternative treatment for the asset's role. Decorative images need
appropriate semantics; informative images need meaningful alternatives.

## Know when to stop

Render with real content, identify the intended point of attention and inspect a connected view. Keep a
change when its mechanism serves the brief without compromising behavior, reading or accessibility. Reject
ornament whose only defense is that it looks less generic. Do not keep adding accents after the chosen
identity is coherent.
