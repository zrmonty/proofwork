# Novel passages: flow to observe, not a voice to copy

Prefer [contemporary examples](contemporary-flow.md) for application copy. Load these passages only for
optional rhythm comparisons. Labels need neither exercise. These selected techniques do not define
typical English or prescribe a literary register.

Read one relevant passage, name the connection between its sentences, then apply that mechanism to the
product's supplied facts and established register. Use an original rewrite. Narrative setup, invented
experience and suspense must not displace an interface's action, state or recovery.

## Sources and reuse

The excerpts below come from version-pinned Standard Ebooks XHTML sources. Their edition notices describe
the originals as believed public domain in the United States and dedicate contributors' work under CC0.
CC0 for editorial contributions is not a blanket worldwide claim about underlying works. Each entry links
its exact text and license notice. Authors and original publication years are identified; punctuation and paragraph breaks
are retained, while inline emphasis and annotations are omitted. Commentary and application guidance are original.


## Conversation advances the information

Jane Austen, *Pride and Prejudice* (1813), chapter 1. [Pinned source](https://github.com/standardebooks/jane-austen_pride-and-prejudice/blob/ed94a32be3e875c0814568050c7544e03aad3ef5/src/epub/text/chapter-1.xhtml); [edition notice](https://github.com/standardebooks/jane-austen_pride-and-prejudice/blob/ed94a32be3e875c0814568050c7544e03aad3ef5/LICENSE.md).

> “My dear Mr. Bennet,” said his lady to him one day, “have you heard that Netherfield Park is let at last?”
>
> Mr. Bennet replied that he had not.
>
> “But it is,” returned she; “for Mrs. Long has just been here, and she told me all about it.”

Notice: a question introduces missing information; the reply answers it; the next turn explains its source. Short and longer turns alternate because their jobs differ.

Transfer: write adjacent prompts, feedback and next steps as connected information. Answer the actual question before introducing another. Keep canonical action names stable. Do not imitate period forms of address, ironic narration or rhetorical questions that have no information need.


## Cause leads naturally to consequence

Charles Dickens, *Great Expectations* (1861), chapter 1. [Pinned source](https://github.com/standardebooks/charles-dickens_great-expectations/blob/81271e3258059ca1f743909edfe6abae6ee2b329/src/epub/text/chapter-1.xhtml); [edition notice](https://github.com/standardebooks/charles-dickens_great-expectations/blob/81271e3258059ca1f743909edfe6abae6ee2b329/LICENSE.md).

> My father’s family name being Pirrip, and my Christian name Philip, my infant tongue could make of both names nothing longer or more explicit than Pip. So, I called myself Pip, and came to be called Pip.

Notice: the first sentence gives the reason for the name; the second resolves it. Repeating Pip and using So preserves the thread instead of introducing a fresh abstraction. The longer setup earns the shorter consequence.

Transfer: connect a known cause to an outcome or supported next step. Keep referents clear across sentences. Do not import autobiographical storytelling, archaic vocabulary or long setup into a control label.


## Concrete action carries the transition

Mark Twain, *The Adventures of Tom Sawyer* (1876), chapter 2. [Pinned source](https://github.com/standardebooks/mark-twain_the-adventures-of-tom-sawyer/blob/d51493e2ec4894468e8cd3b38f848d4fae4e78b9/src/epub/text/chapter-2.xhtml); [edition notice](https://github.com/standardebooks/mark-twain_the-adventures-of-tom-sawyer/blob/d51493e2ec4894468e8cd3b38f848d4fae4e78b9/LICENSE.md).

> Tom appeared on the sidewalk with a bucket of whitewash and a long-handled brush. He surveyed the fence, and all gladness left him and a deep melancholy settled down upon his spirit.

Notice: named objects establish what Tom is doing. The next sentence reacts to that same scene; He and the fence carry the connection without an explanatory section heading. Details do work before any general claim.

Transfer: name the actual object and action, then state what changed or remains possible. Prefer supplied specifics to praise. Do not add melodrama, fictional emotions or sensory decoration to an error message.


## Specific detail changes the pace

Willa Cather, *My Ántonia* (1918), Book I, chapter 1. [Pinned source](https://github.com/standardebooks/willa-cather_my-antonia/blob/9e7c26a2f2954ed707528ce427172f970a77e6c9/src/epub/text/chapter-1-1.xhtml); [edition notice](https://github.com/standardebooks/willa-cather_my-antonia/blob/9e7c26a2f2954ed707528ce427172f970a77e6c9/LICENSE.md).

> We went all the way in day-coaches, becoming more sticky and grimy with each stage of the journey. Jake bought everything the newsboys offered him: candy, oranges, brass collar buttons, a watch-charm, and for me a Life of Jesse James, which I remember as one of the most satisfactory books I have ever read.

Notice: the first sentence moves the journey forward; the next slows down with selected purchases and a remembered book. Concrete detail supports a distinct voice rather than a sequence of generic claims. Sentence length follows attention.

Transfer: use task-relevant supplied detail and vary explanatory rhythm when it helps reading. A supported example can make a help passage clearer. Do not invent personal memories, overload a label with lists or treat historical vocabulary as a modern voice template.


## Check the application rewrite

Does each sentence develop the previous one, and does its detail help the current task? Preserve meaningful
repetition rather than replacing every noun with a synonym. Read the encounter order, then recheck certainty,
permissions and recovery against the message contract. Keep useful writing even when it contains a long
sentence, a short fragment or a real list. Source examples cannot prove reader satisfaction or UI behavior.
